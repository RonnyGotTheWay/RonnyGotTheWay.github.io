Overview

This project investigates whether patients with diabetes and hypertension follow distinct longitudinal care pathways. The central question is whether a hidden subgroup of patients silently disengages from routine care and later re-enters the health system through emergency department or inpatient services. We transformed longitudinal encounter histories into behavioral sequences, engineered patient-level features, applied clustering, and profiled clinically meaningful patient segments.

The analysis turned absence of care into an observable signal, making long silent periods actionable from a chronic disease care management perspective.

Dataset and Preparation

The data came from the Stormont Vail Health integrated encounter master table and focused on chronic disease patients with diabetes and hypertension from 2022-2025. The final analytic table covered 46,714 patients and 3,073,410 unique encounters.

A key preparation issue was duplicate EncounterKey rows caused by diagnosis-level joins. I deduplicated the data to one record per true encounter before sequence construction. Without deduplication, visit frequency and gap metrics would be artificially inflated, distorting the care pathway profiles.

Analytical Design

Module 1

Sequence Construction

Encoded each encounter as a behavioral event: routine visit = 1, specialist visit = 2, ED or inpatient acute-care event = 3, and silence marker = 0 when the gap between visits exceeded 90 days. This treated absence of care as an analytically visible signal.

Module 2

Feature Engineering

Derived six patient-level features: total encounters, acute-care ratio, mean gap between visits, gap variability, maximum silence duration, and probability that a silence period is followed by an acute event.

Module 3

Clustering

Standardized the six features, applied K-Means clustering, selected a four-cluster solution based on the elbow method and clinical interpretability, and generated cluster summaries and visual diagnostics.

Module 4

Clinical Interpretation

Compared care pathway groups by encounter pattern, acute-care use, mortality, MyChart activation, and SDOH risk indicators. The most operationally important subgroup was patients with unstable post-silence acute return.

Best insight

Best Insight: Silence Is a Predictive Intervention Window

The strongest finding is that the highest-risk patients are not simply those with the most visits. Instead, risk concentrates among patients whose care trajectories become unstable after long silent periods. In the acute-unstable cluster, nearly six out of ten post-silence transitions ended in emergency or inpatient care, suggesting that silence in the clinical record is not a benign gap but a predictive intervention window.

Cluster Interpretation

Cluster Share Interpretation Operational signal
Cluster 0 30.4% High-touch continuous management with frequent visits and short gaps. Very low probability of silence ending in acute care.
Cluster 1 53.2% Stable management, the largest group, with moderate encounter volume. Relatively low acute risk.
Cluster 2 4.7% Acute-unstable post-silence return with highest ED/inpatient ratio and highest mortality. Lowest MyChart activation and 59% probability that silence ends in acute care.
Cluster 3 11.6% Low-engagement, long-silence group with very long silence periods. Comparatively lower acute escalation than Cluster 2.
